Modelling predator-prey interactions

Tutorial 2 · Level 1 · Predator-prey. Adapted for Simile on the web from the Simulistics tutorial by Robert Muetzelfeldt.

This tutorial works through the classic predator-prey model: rabbits and foxes. It begins with the textbook (Lotka-Volterra) form, recasts the diagram so that it says more about the biology, and then replaces two of the textbook's simplistic assumptions with relationships sketched by hand. A bonus section shows how to enter a model given as differential equations.

It assumes you have done Tutorial 1, Bank account, and know how to add stocks, flows, variables and influences, open an element's properties dialog, and run a model.

Stage 1: Modelling the dynamics of a single population

Step 1

Choose File › New model, then File › Rename model… and call it Predator-prey.

Step 2

Add two stocks to the diagram, one above the other, and name them rabbits and foxes.

Step 3

Draw a flow into the rabbits stock, from empty space on its left, and name it rabbit_repro.

Step 4

Draw a flow out of the rabbits stock, into empty space on its right, and name it predation.

Step 5

Draw an influence arrow:

  • from the rabbits stock to the rabbit_repro flow;
  • from the rabbits stock to the predation flow;
  • from the foxes stock to the predation flow.

Remember that an influence into a flow ends on its valve. Your model diagram should now look like this:

Step 6

Enter the following expression for the rabbit_repro flow: 0.5*rabbits

Step 7

Enter the following expression for the predation flow: 0.01*rabbits*foxes

Step 8

Set the initial value for the rabbits stock to 5000.

Step 9

Set the initial value for the foxes stock to 45.

Everything on the diagram is now black. (The foxes stock has no flows yet, so it simply stays at 45.)

Step 10

In the Simulation window, click load to build the model.

Step 11

Change the run settings:

  • set Execute for to 50;
  • leave Time step(s) at 0.1.

Step 12

Set up a plotter for the rabbits stock: Widgets › Plotter, choose rabbits, and click add.

Step 13

Run the model with ▶.

Note that the rabbit population grows exponentially: there is nothing to hold it in check. With 45 foxes, predation takes 0.45 of the rabbits a year while reproduction adds 0.5, so the population grows by about 5% a year, to just over 60,000 after 50 years.

Stage 2: Adding in predator dynamics

Step 1

Add a flow into the foxes stock, and name it fox_repro.

Step 2

Add a flow out of the foxes stock, and name it fox_mortality.

Step 3

Draw an influence from the predation flow (its valve) to the fox_repro flow.

This reflects the assumption in the standard textbook model that the predators' reproduction is proportional to the rate of predation on the prey.

Step 4

Draw an influence from the foxes stock to the fox_mortality flow: the number of foxes dying depends on the number of foxes.

Your model diagram should now look like this:

Step 5

Enter the following expression for the fox_repro flow: 0.01*predation

Step 6

Enter the following expression for the fox_mortality flow: 0.2*foxes

Step 7

Set up a second plotter, for the foxes stock: Widgets › Plotter again, choose foxes, and click add.

You need a separate plotter because the number of foxes is a couple of orders of magnitude less than the number of rabbits: on the same graph, the line for the foxes would lie along the bottom.

The two plotters share the Displays area as tabs. To see both at once, click the Arrange contents button on the Displays titlebar (the first of its buttons) and choose Tiled side by side.

Step 8

Run the model again with ▶. You have changed the model since it was loaded, so the run control loads it again first; your run settings and your plots are kept.

To see the model and its results side by side, choose View › Arrange windows › This model beside its simulation.

You should now see that the rabbit and fox populations cycle: the rabbit population crashes as the fox population increases, followed by a crash in the fox population, after which the rabbits recover again.

Stage 3: Re-casting the model diagram

The present model is based on a number of assumptions that are biologically unsound. For example, it assumes that the number of rabbits eaten per fox is directly proportional to the number of rabbits, so doubling the number of rabbits doubles the number eaten per fox — even if there are millions of rabbits around!

We will tackle the problem of making the model more biologically realistic in two stages. First, we will change the model diagram a little, adding a couple of variables and changing the influence arrows, but keeping the same mathematical structure: the revised model should produce exactly the same results. These changes give our model better handles for expressing biologically meaningful relationships. Then, in the following stage, we will replace some of the simplistic assumptions with others that are biologically more realistic.

Step 1

Remove the influence arrows from rabbits to predation, and from predation to fox_repro. To remove an arrow, click on it to select it — it turns blue — and press Delete.

(Pressing on an arrow and dragging bends it instead; a click without dragging selects it.)

Step 2

Add two new variables, naming them rfox and eaten_per_fox.

Step 3

Draw influence arrows:

  • from the rabbits stock to eaten_per_fox;
  • from eaten_per_fox to rfox;
  • from eaten_per_fox to the predation flow;
  • from rfox to the fox_repro flow; and
  • from the foxes stock to the fox_repro flow.

Your model diagram should now look something like this:

Step 4

Enter the following expression for each variable or flow:

  • eaten_per_fox: 0.01*rabbits
  • rfox: 0.01*eaten_per_fox
  • predation: eaten_per_fox*foxes
  • fox_repro: rfox*foxes

Satisfy yourself that these changes have not altered the mathematical nature of the model.

Step 5

Run the model again with ▶, confirming that you do indeed get the same behaviour that you had before.

Stage 4: Sketching biologically meaningful relationships

What we now have are some variables in our model (eaten_per_fox and rfox) for introducing biologically meaningful relationships. The influence arrows we have drawn to these two variables express the biologically reasonable assumptions that:

  • the number of rabbits eaten per fox, eaten_per_fox, depends on the number of rabbits; and
  • the number of baby foxes produced per fox, rfox, depends on the number of rabbits eaten per fox.

So far, however, we have kept the simplistic assumptions of the conventional model: that the number of rabbits eaten per fox is directly proportional to the number of rabbits, and that the rate of reproduction per fox is directly proportional to the number of rabbits eaten. In reality, both assumptions are way off the mark: if there are lots of rabbits around, foxes reach a limit to how many they can eat. Similarly, however many rabbits foxes eat, there is an upper limit to the number of offspring they can produce in a year.

How can we incorporate these refinements? We have two options. One is to find some mathematical expression that better captures our understanding. If you are mathematically inclined, you might like to give that a try. The other is to use Simile's ability to sketch a graph of the relationship between two variables, and that is what we will do here.

Step 1

Open the properties dialog for the variable eaten_per_fox, and delete the existing expression.

Step 2

Click the Graph tab.

The graph pad shows a flat line across the middle, made of 21 points joined by straight lines. The scale of each axis is set by the boxes at its ends: Y max and Y min beside the pad, X min and X max beneath it. As you move the mouse over the pad, the position under it is shown underneath.

Step 3

Scale the X axis, which stands for the number of rabbits: enter 10000 in the X max box, at the right-hand end beneath the pad.

Step 4

Scale the Y axis, the number of rabbits eaten per fox per year: enter 100 in the Y max box, at the top beside the pad.

Step 5

Move the mouse into the pad and drag across it, holding the mouse button down. Notice how the line follows the mouse: every point you pass over takes the height of the mouse. (A single click sets just the nearest point.)

Step 6

Adjust it so that it passes through the origin at (0,0), and rises smoothly until it reaches a plateau, looking something like this:

When the model runs, Simile uses linear interpolation to work out the value on the Y axis from the value on the X axis. Beyond the ends of the X axis it holds the end values: that is what Out of range: Truncate means.

Step 7

Click Back to the equation. (The Graph tab has no OK button: the graph is only half the job until the equation uses it, so you OK the dialog from the Equation tab.) Because the expression was empty, and eaten_per_fox has only one influence, Simile has entered graph(rabbits): the value of the sketched graph at the current number of rabbits.

Click OK.

Step 8

Open the properties dialog for the variable rfox, delete the existing expression, and click the Graph tab.

We are now going to sketch the relationship between rfox, the number of foxes produced per fox per year, and eaten_per_fox, the number of rabbits eaten per fox per year.

Step 9

Scale it, setting X max to 100 and Y max to 1.

Note that the X max value (100) is the same as the Y max value in the previous graph. This is because they refer to the same quantity: the number of rabbits eaten per fox per year. The Y max of 1 says that a fox cannot produce more than one young a year.

Step 10

Sketch a graph looking something like this:

Before reading on, try to work out for yourself what this says… It shows that a fox does not reproduce at all when its food intake is below some lower threshold. Above that, its rate of reproduction rises in proportion to the extra food it eats, up to a plateau: the most young it can produce in a year.

Step 11

Click Back to the equation: the expression now reads graph(eaten_per_fox). Click OK.

Step 12

Run the model again with ▶.

You should notice a big difference in the shape of the graphs. With the sketches above, the foxes first eat the rabbits down to a handful; then, with little to eat, the foxes dwindle; the rabbits, freed from predation, boom; the foxes boom after them — and then the rabbits plunge below zero, which no population can do.

Why the rabbits went negative The model moves forward in steps of 0.1 years. At the height of the fox boom, the foxes eat more rabbits in one step than there are, so the stock overshoots past zero; a negative stock then breeds “negative rabbits” ever faster. It is an artefact of the time step, not of the biology. Set Time step(s) to 0.01, click ■ to go back to the start, and run again: the rabbits now fall to zero and stay there, and the foxes, with nothing left to eat, die away after them.

Your own sketches will not be exactly these, and small differences in them can make large differences to the results: try redrawing either graph and running again.

Stage 5: Bonus section: re-visiting the classic predator-prey model

In this tutorial, I began by sticking faithfully to the mathematical form of the traditional Lotka-Volterra predator-prey model, but designed the System Dynamics diagram to put more emphasis on biological processes. So I used four flows, for the reproduction and mortality of the two populations, even though these were not explicitly mentioned in the original model: all it had were two rate-of-change equations. This reflects my own attitude to modelling: that it should begin as a conceptual design activity, rather than being driven by mathematical notation.

Nevertheless, there will be times when you come across a model expressed as a set of differential equations, and you feel you have neither the inclination nor the ability to re-express it in terms of biological processes, represented as separate flows. What can you do?

Well, it is really pretty straightforward. All you have to do is to follow four simple steps:

  1. Create one stock for each state variable.
  2. Make a single inflow for each stock.
  3. Draw influence arrows from stocks to inflows, as required by the differential equations.
  4. Enter each differential equation into the flow for the corresponding state variable.

Let's see this in action.

Step 1

Start a new model with File › New model, and rename it Lotka-Volterra.

Step 2

Add two stocks, and name them X and Y.

Step 3

Add a single flow into each stock, and name them dXdt and dYdt.

Step 4

Draw influence arrows from both stocks to both flows (four arrows in total). Your model diagram should now look like this:

Step 5

Enter the following expressions into the flows:

  • dXdt: 0.5*X - 0.01*X*Y
  • dYdt: 0.01*0.01*X*Y - 0.2*Y

and give the stocks the same initial values as before: 5000 for X and 45 for Y.

Step 6

Load and run the model, with Execute for 50 and a time step of 0.1, plotting X and Y.

The results are exactly those of Stage 2: X plays the rabbits and Y the foxes. The two diagrams are two ways of writing down the same mathematics, but only one of them says anything about rabbits and foxes.
